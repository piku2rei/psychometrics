01 / Start with the goal

First, name the skill you want to measure.

A construct is the skill or idea a test is meant to measure. It is the target behind the score.

The target

Demand-shock reasoning

A student can trace a demand shift through to equilibrium and explain why price and quantity change.

Not quite the same

Remembering a definition

A student can repeat what “demand” means. That knowledge may help, but it is not the whole target if the goal is to apply a model.

Design question: What should a student be able to do with the idea after learning it?

02 / The score is indirect

We see an answer; we infer the skill.

You can read a student's written answer. You cannot directly see the reasoning skill behind it. So a score is evidence—a clue we use to make an inference, or a reasoned judgment.

Why one question can mislead: a student might know the idea but misread the wording, or guess correctly without understanding. Several well-chosen questions give a fuller set of clues.

03 / Reliability

Reliability asks: are the results consistent?

Reliability is the consistency of scores when we measure under similar conditions. In this target picture, each dot is one result from an equivalent short quiz.

Target centre = the intended result. Dots = scores from similar attempts.

04 / Validity is a different question

Validity asks: are we measuring the intended skill?

Validity is how well evidence supports the meaning we want to give a score. Here, the centre is the goal: applying a demand shock, not just recalling a term.

Same target idea. The selected quiz task changes where the results cluster.

What does the quiz ask students to do?
ScatteredTightly grouped

Consistent scores—but recall is not the application goal.

The distinction: a quiz can consistently test recall, yet its scores are weak evidence that students can apply a model. A better-aligned task asks them to explain a new demand shock and its effect on equilibrium.

The target is a metaphor. Reliability and validity are distinct but connected: consistent scores can support a validity argument, but consistency alone does not prove the interpretation. Real validity work gathers evidence for a specific score use.

06 / Put the ideas to work

Write the question to match the learning goal.

Learning goal

Given a market change, explain how a demand shift changes equilibrium price and quantity.

Recall-only version

“What is demand?”

This may check a useful definition. By itself, it does not show that a student can apply a demand shock and reason through the equilibrium change.

Better-aligned version

“Assume café meals are a normal good. Average income rises, with other factors unchanged. Show the demand shift, predict the new equilibrium price and quantity, and explain why.”

The student must apply the idea, show the change, and explain the chain of reasoning.

Quick check: Which question gives better evidence for the stated goal?

After writing, check that the scoring guide rewards the explanation you asked for—not only a memorized phrase.
